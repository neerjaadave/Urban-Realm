LEED APMS Urban Design · Georgia TechJacobs · San FranciscoKathiawadiWriter · Traveller

From the pols and chowks of Kathiawad to master plans across three continents — I design cities for people, and write about them for anyone willing to look closely.

Who I Am

I am a Project Manager and Urban Designer at Jacobs, San Francisco, leading complex, multidisciplinary projects across large-scale urban districts, institutional and corporate campuses, data centers, and advanced manufacturing facilities. With formal training in Urban Design and Architecture, I bring expertise in delivering design vision with efficiency and operational excellence.

My design philosophy centres on community-first principles, drawing from the 15-minute city framework and climate resilience strategies. My graduate research with Professor Ellen Dunham-Jones — Director of Urban Design at Georgia Tech — on autonomous public transit and micromobility informed innovative solutions to first- and last-mile connectivity in suburban Atlanta.

Where I Come From

I grew up in Kathiawad — the Saurashtra peninsula of Gujarat, a place of extraordinary built heritage. I studied architecture at the Indubhai Parekh School of Architecture in Rajkot, before a diploma in Built Heritage Conservation Science at J.J. College of Architecture, Mumbai, and an MS in Urban Design at Georgia Institute of Technology, Atlanta.

Kathiawadi towns gave me my first lessons in urbanism long before I had the vocabulary for it: the chowk at the centre, the pol neighbourhood system, the jharokhas projecting from every facade. Cities are not neutral containers — they are active participants in shaping behaviour, identity, and power.

Professional Work

At Jacobs (2021–Present), I lead projects spanning urban design, master planning, and project management across the USA and internationally — from Transit Oriented Development for LA Metro and Phoenix, to urban design for the Diriyah Gate in Riyadh, to the LA 2028 Olympic Games Mobility Concept Plan, to semiconductor campus master planning.

Previously at Studio H (SHAPE), Atlanta, I led the Microsoft Campus Master Plan (70 acres), Paschals' Campus Redevelopment, and trauma-informed housing design. Earlier, I founded The Studio Re — my own practice — working on projects from eco-resorts in Bihar to sports cafes in Rajkot.

Research & Teaching

As a Graduate Research Assistant at Georgia Tech, I contributed to best practices for autonomous shuttle user experience as a Georgia Smart Communities Challenge scholar. I have also been a Research Associate at CEPT University, Ahmedabad, and taught Architectural Design and History of Architecture at Marwadi University, Rajkot.

What This Blog Is About

This blog is the personal side of a professional life spent thinking about cities — written freely, from the perspective of a Kathiawadi woman who has lived and worked across India, the USA, and beyond.

Heritage · Public Space · Gujarat

Ur-bane

By Neerja Dave November 30, 2015 JunagadhCivic ArchitecturePublic Realm

History is the only means by which one can travel to the past. Built heritage holds enormous value in terms of encompassing facets of an entire era within itself. Older parts of our cities are frozen fractals. Each city from the pre-World War era emerged as one that was dominated by its public architecture.

The city of Junagadh pre-dates written historical records as an ancient Greek regnum. Following which a streak of Hindu kings laid their regime over it till the 18th century, when the Muslim Nawabs ruled for a couple of decades and — under the influence of the British Raj — up to 1947. Magnificent edifices were constructed during this phase including the Aina Mahal, Rang Mahal, Circle Chowk, and the Lord Ray Gate.

Rang Mahal, Junagadh
The Rang Mahal — one of the final surviving excerpts of public architecture from the Nawabi era.

The Public Realm is out-rightly seconded in priority to the profit-oriented real estate market. Classical notions of city planning have long been replaced by modern and now post-modern ideas of designing our cities.

These are but the final few excerpts of public architecture that can be found in the city. The city boasts of a very strong architectural backdrop — dominated by its public spaces and civic buildings. Whereas modern developments build an image of faceless buildings and unusable public spaces, if any at all.

This is a fundamental issue with our modern planning techniques. The Public Realm is outright seconded in priority to the profit-oriented real estate market. During the transition, the focus on providing spaces for the burgeoning class has shifted drastically — which, if it continues, shall render our cities faceless and without an identity of their own.

Divan Chowk, Junagadh
Divan Chowk today — dirty pavements, haphazardly parked automobiles, and nobody who could guess this was once adjacent to a grand palace.
The Organic Fort City

The fort city has developed organically, with each urban fragment responding to its topography, climate, and community settlement patterns. All public structures were placed on topographic high-points and laid a strong axis that cut through the city — wherein all major city crossroads consisted of a hierarchy of scaled urban public spaces, or chowks.

The King's Road, Junagadh
The King's Road — the ceremonial spine of the city, consisting of all major public structures and civic architecture built during the Nawabi era.
What Could Have Been

Today one can only imagine what the city of Junagadh could have been, had these places of historical and public importance been maintained and restored to their previous glorious selves.

As infrastructure and the real estate market have taken over as the key determinants of our urban-scapes, it is but difficult to come across an intent to build public architecture. Efforts must be laid to formulate an evolved typology of civic architecture that satisfies the real estate market while simultaneously sufficing the need of a city to gain an identity of its own.

Policies must be formulated by governing authorities to enrich urban pockets with a well-placed network of civic spaces and buildings — before the last frozen fractals of our heritage cities thaw away entirely.

Urban Edge as a Binding Element

A Case Study Analysis of Race Course, Rajkot

By Neerja Dave January 17, 2020 RajkotPublic SpaceUrban Edge

Rajkot city is located in the central part of the Saurashtra Peninsula in the western part of India. And at its heart — binding the medieval town with its post-industrial growth — sits Race Course: a 100-acre public park that has become one of the most quietly successful urban spaces in the country.

Importance in Urban Setting

Race Course binds the entire city together due to its placement at the junction of the medieval part of the town and the post-industrial developments. It sits in very close proximity to all three transport stations of the city — the airport, the railway station, and the City Bus Stand — a location that puts this public space in a uniquely democratic position.

In its beginning phase, Race Course was set up by the British Raj as a horse racing facility. Post Indian Independence, the 100-acre area was converted into a city park — a transformation that tells you everything about what a city chooses to do with inherited colonial space.

The park now houses sports amenities, gardens, a children's park, an art gallery, an amusement park, a planetarium, a library, and maidaans — free open spaces — to hold festive gatherings, commercial expo events, and an informal children's recreation zone.

Composition of Urban Space

Race Course — Ring Road, as it is popularly referred to — is a mix-bag of land uses. A vital part of the Indian economy is its informal sector, and Race Course, with its 100-acre potential, provides all necessary urban equipment and enables hawkers and street vendors to sell their services and goods in a very dignified manner.

Trees are a major element. One of the intangible reasons why people are drawn towards this place is the microclimate. Rajkot, being situated in a semi-arid, hot-dry climate where temperatures rise to 40–43 degrees Celsius, benefits enormously from the significant tree cover. It is measurably cooler during the daytime, and evenings are very pleasant.

Race Course, Rajkot
Race Course — the 100-acre city park whose compound wall has become one of the most democratic urban edges in the Saurashtra region.
The Compound Wall — A Design Lesson

Race Course has been composed as a congregation of related and supporting land-use parcels along its 4 kilometres of perimeter. The compound wall design was conceived specifically for users to sit in comfortable, clean, shade-providing space. This critical design decision brought the entire city — across all age groups, social divisions, and economic classes — to one solid ground of usability and recreation.

The compound wall acts as a binding element for communities and economic classes alike — a democratic edge, designed by an architect who understood that affordability and dignity are not in opposition.

The Architect Behind the Edge

The architect commissioned to design the urban edge was Mr. Indubhai Parekh — a very revered figure in the architectural history of the Saurashtra peninsula. He had the task of minimising the building cost while providing maximum usability through the edge. He specialised in affordable building practices, and hence the design translated in the compound wall of Race Course was one that held vernacular spatial traits while fitting well within financial constraints.

Morning walkers throng the space starting from 4am up to 7am. Hawkers sell healthy soups and snacks. At noon, the edge is used by commuters to nap in the shade. Many employees from surrounding offices use it as a lunching spot. But evenings are when the space truly comes to life, with the entire city and people of all age groups flocking to the park.

Indubhai Parekh designed something that textbooks rarely teach: a public edge that costs almost nothing to maintain, generates its own informal economy, and serves every demographic simultaneously. That is not luck. That is profound urban design intelligence.
